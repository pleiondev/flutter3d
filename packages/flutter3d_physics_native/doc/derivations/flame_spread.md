# How fast a flame's edge creeps over oak, pine, cardboard and straw

The core creeps a fire's edge sideways and down at the LIFT test's
opposed-flow rate (Quintiere and Harkleroad, NBSIR 84-2943, 1984),

<math display="block"><mrow><mi>V</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mi>Φ</mi><mrow><mi>k</mi><mi>ρ</mi><mi>c</mi><mspace width="0.2em"/><msup><mrow><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>ig</mtext></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>T</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>)</mo></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mfrac></mrow></math>

over a surface at *T*<sub>s</sub> no cooler than *T*<sub>s,min</sub>, and
lights a heated spot in the time a thick solid takes,
*t* = (π/4)·*kρc*·(Δ*T*/*q*'')². The LIFT test measures Φ,
*T*<sub>s,min</sub>, *kρc* and *T*<sub>ig</sub> together, but its open
tables hold wood products only, not solid oak, pine, cardboard or straw
(gaps G1 and G7 of the research notes). This says what each preset takes
and why.

## Oak and pine: plywood's Φ, with each species' own *kρc* and *T*<sub>ig</sub>

Quintiere's thermal theory of opposed spread writes Φ from the flame
alone: the heat flux *q*''<sub>f</sub> it lays ahead of its edge over the
length δ<sub>f</sub> it heats,

<math display="block"><mrow><mi>Φ</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mn>4</mn><mi>π</mi></mfrac><msubsup><mi>q</mi><mi>f</mi><mrow><mo>″</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></msubsup><msub><mi>δ</mi><mi>f</mi></msub></mrow></math>

(Quintiere, *Fundamentals of Fire Phenomena*, §8.4). The solid is in *kρc*
and *T*<sub>ig</sub>; Φ is the flame's. A flame over oak and a flame over
pine are wood flames over a charring cellulosic surface, as over plywood.
The LIFT tables show how far Φ moves across wood products: 12.91
kW²/m³ for plywood 1.27 cm, 12.75 for Douglas-fir particleboard, 7.49 for
plywood 0.635 cm, which is thin (NBSIR 84-2943, table 2). OAK and PINE take
the thick plywood row's **Φ = 12.91 kW²/m³**, with their own measured *kρc*
and *T*<sub>ig</sub> (Tran and White, *Fire and Materials* 16, 1992,
table 3): over a cold surface red oak's edge creeps at

<math display="block"><mrow><mfrac><mrow><mn>12.91</mn><mo>×</mo><msup><mn>10</mn><mn>6</mn></msup></mrow><mrow><mn>0.360</mn><mo>×</mo><msup><mn>10</mn><mn>6</mn></msup><mo>×</mo><msup><mn>295</mn><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mfrac><mo>=</mo><mn>0.41</mn><mspace width="0.2em"/><mtext>mm/s</mtext></mrow></math>

and southern pine's (*kρc* 0.183, 300 K short of 593 K) at 0.78 mm/s,
against plywood's 0.17 mm/s (WOOD, *kρc* 0.54, 370 K short of 663 K). The coolest surface it creeps over is
plywood's too, **120 °C**: the wood products measured lie at 120 to 210 °C,
and the lowest keeps a species' edge from stopping where a wood product's
did not.

If the flame's flux scaled as (*T*<sub>f</sub> − *T*<sub>ig</sub>), as de
Ris's laminar theory has it, oak's Φ would come out above plywood's by
((*T*<sub>f</sub> − 588)/(*T*<sub>f</sub> − 663))², 1.12 for a flame at
1930 K: well inside the factor of two the wood products spread over.

## Cardboard: *kρc* from the times it took to catch

Corrugated board catches at 350 °C, what its critical flux of 8.5 kW/m²
gives a black surface (Khan, de Ris and Ogden, *Fire Safety Science* 9,
2008). Under the cone it took 30 to 32.5 s to catch at 25 kW/m², 6.75 to 8
at 50 and 3 to 4.25 at 75 (UL FSRI Materials and Products Database,
Corrugated Cardboard): times that fall as the square of the flux, as a
thick solid's do. A thick solid past its critical flux catches after

<math display="block"><mrow><msup><mi>t</mi><mrow><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>/</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></msup><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mn>2</mn><msqrt><mi>π</mi></msqrt></mfrac><mfrac><mrow><msup><mi>q</mi><mo>″</mo></msup><mo>−</mo><msubsup><mi>q</mi><mtext>cr</mtext><mo>″</mo></msubsup></mrow><mrow><msqrt><mrow><mi>k</mi><mi>ρ</mi><mi>c</mi></mrow></msqrt><mspace width="0.2em"/><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>ig</mtext></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>T</mi><mi>a</mi></msub><mo>)</mo></mrow></mfrac></mrow></math>

the form the core uses, its losses at ignition subtracted from the flux.
Fitting *t*<sup>−1/2</sup> against *q*'' − 8.5 kW/m² through the origin by
least squares over the three means, 31.25, 7.375 and 3.625 s, gives a slope
of 8.285 × 10⁻⁶ m²/(W s<sup>1/2</sup>) and

<math display="block"><mrow><mi>k</mi><mi>ρ</mi><mi>c</mi><mo>=</mo><msup><mrow><mo>(</mo><mfrac><mn>2</mn><mrow><msqrt><mi>π</mi></msqrt><mo>×</mo><mn>8.285</mn><mo>×</mo><msup><mn>10</mn><mrow><mo>−</mo><mn>6</mn></mrow></msup><mo>×</mo><mn>330</mn></mrow></mfrac><mo>)</mo></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mn>1.70</mn><mo>×</mo><msup><mn>10</mn><mn>5</mn></msup><mspace width="0.2em"/><mtext>J²/(m⁴ K² s)</mtext></mrow></math>

It gives back 8.5 s at 50 kW/m² and 3.3 s at 75, and 54 s at 25 against
the 31 measured: near its critical flux the board catches sooner than a
thick solid would. A fit with no critical flux holds all three within 4 %
and gives 2.44 × 10⁵, but the core does subtract the losses, and with that
value would take 10.6 s at 50 kW/m². A flame held to a board in the core,
whose losses there are its radiation alone, catches it in 7.4 s at
50 kW/m², inside the measured 6.75 to 8.

Its edge creeps as over the thin plywood row, **Φ = 7.49 kW²/m³, no cooler
than 170 °C** (NBSIR 84-2943, table 2): the nearest thin cellulosic
product measured.

## Straw: Φ from Rothermel's spread with no wind

A straw bed has no LIFT measurement, but Rothermel's model gives its spread
over flat ground in still air, where the fire backs into the fuel as an
opposed-flow edge does,

(USDA INT-115, 1972, eq. 52 with no wind or slope). For the straw Rossa,
Davim and Fernandes burnt (*Fire* 9, 94, 2026, table 1) — σ = 5265 /m =
1605 /ft, particle density 285 kg/m³, a bed of 15.0 kg/m³ holding
0.39 kg/m², oven-dry — and Rothermel's own constants for every fuel (heat
content 8000 Btu/lb, mineral 0.0555 and 0.010 silica-free, *Q*<sub>ig</sub>
250 Btu/lb when dry; INT-115, table 1 and eq. 12):

The core creeps a bed's edge with the bed's own *kρc*: THATCH takes that of
Rossa's bed, its conductivity a straw bale's,
λ = 0.0444 + 2.72 × 10⁻⁴ ρ W/(m K) (Costes and colleagues, *Buildings* 7,
11, 2017, eq. 2), at 15 kg/m³, 0.0485 W/(m K) — below the 68 to 123 kg/m³
the bales were measured over, where the intercept, near still air's own
conductivity with radiation across the voids, dominates — and its specific
heat 1938 J/(kg K) (`heat_of_gasification.md`):

*R*<sub>0</sub>:

Rothermel's no-wind spread does not stop over cold fuel, so THATCH has no

## What is left out

- Rothermel's model is built on a particle density of 512.6 kg/m³ for every
  fuel; the bed here takes straw's own, 285, so its packing ratio is
  Rossa's measured 0.053.
- The straw's time to catch under the cone, 9.5 to 18.75 s at 35 kW/m²
  (UL FSRI, Straw), is several times what its thin stalks need to reach
  320 °C, ρ<sub>p</sub>*c*Δ*T*/(σ*q*'') under 1 s: those times are of a
  sustained flame over a packed sample, which needs its gas to build up.
  They are not used.
